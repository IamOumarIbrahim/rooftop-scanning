\begin{abstract}
Before paying for a solar design study, a building owner needs a quick first answer: how much solar power fits on the roof, and does it pay back? In the United Arab Emirates, most automated tools cannot give this answer, because roof laser scans and 3D city models are not public. We present SolarScan, an open-source tool that uses free data only. It takes an address, finds the building outline in OpenStreetMap, lays out the solar modules on the roof, and estimates the yearly energy and the payback time. To test it, we traced the roofs of \NBuildings{} buildings in \NEmirates{} emirates by hand. When the map outline matched the building (\NOsmOk{} of \NManual), the roof area was within \OkMape\% of the trace on average. The other \NOsmBad{} outlines were wrong: part of the building was missing, open ground or a courtyard was counted as roof, or the roof had several levels. For a university building, the tool estimates \CasePdc~kWp, \CaseEnergy~MWh per year and a payback of \CasePayback{} years. Cost and electricity price change the payback most; the panel direction changes it by only \CaseAlLoss\%.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Rooftop photovoltaic (PV) systems suit the United Arab Emirates (UAE) well: at the University of Sharjah, each square meter of flat ground receives about \Ghi~kWh of sunlight per year. Yet many projects stop at the first question: \emph{is this roof worth a closer look?} To answer it, an installer usually visits the site. This takes time and money, and an owner of many buildings, such as a school group or a logistics company, needs the answer for all of them at once.

Automated tools exist, but they need data that the UAE does not have. Large studies use airborne light detection and ranging (LiDAR) or 3D city models \cite{walch2020, gagnon2018}. Others use land-registry data with machine learning \cite{assouline2018}. Commercial services use licensed images and charge per request. None of these gives a free first answer in the Gulf, although rooftop PV can be economically viable in the UAE \cite{griffiths2016, alhammami2021}.

OpenStreetMap (OSM) is a free world map made by volunteers. It contains building outlines \cite{haklay2008}, but their quality varies from place to place \cite{hecht2013, fan2014, brovelli2018, biljecki2023}. The Photovoltaic Geographical Information System (PVGIS) of the European Commission gives free solar energy estimates for any point in Europe, Africa and the Middle East \cite{huld2012}. Together, the two sources give a roof and the energy it can produce.

We built a tool on this idea and asked three questions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Q1:} How close is the OSM roof area to the real roof?
\item \textbf{Q2:} What does a full screening report look like for a real building?
\item \textbf{Q3:} Which inputs change the answer most?
\end{itemize}

The paper makes three contributions. First, SolarScan, an open-source tool that goes from an address to a report using only OSM and PVGIS. Second, a public test of OSM roof areas on \NBuildings{} UAE buildings, with checked hand traces of the roofs and a description of how OSM outlines fail. Third, a sensitivity study that shows which inputs matter.

\section{Related Work}

Table~\ref{tab:prior} groups earlier work by its main data source. Reviews describe many methods that use geographic information systems (GIS) to estimate rooftop PV potential \cite{freitas2015, gassar2021}. Most of them need LiDAR, 3D building models or city data. Deep-learning methods find roofs or panels in aerial images \cite{yu2018}, but they need large image sets and strong computers. Energy models such as pvlib \cite{holmgren2018} and PVGIS \cite{huld2012} are accurate, but the user must describe the roof. Mainzer \emph{et al.} \cite{mainzer2014} used OSM to estimate the solar potential of a whole country, and later combined OSM outlines with aerial images to find roof planes in a city \cite{mainzer2017}. Joshi \emph{et al.} \cite{joshi2021} mapped rooftop PV worldwide from building footprints and machine learning; they name the footprints as the largest source of error. To our knowledge, no open tool turns one address into a screening report, and no study has checked OSM roof areas for this use in the UAE.

\section{Method}

SolarScan works in six steps: find the building, measure the roof, remove the edge strip, lay out the modules, estimate the energy and estimate the payback. Fig.~\ref{fig:hero} shows the result for one building. Table~\ref{tab:assume} lists the default values and their sources. The user can change every value in a settings file.

\subsection{Find the Building}
The user gives an address or coordinates. The tool asks OSM for all buildings within 250~m. It selects the building that contains the point or, if there is none, the closest one. If OSM has no building there, the tool stops with an error message. It never invents an outline.

\subsection{Measure the Roof}
OSM stores each corner of the outline as latitude $\phi$ and longitude $\lambda$. We convert them to meters on a flat plane around the center of the building $(\phi_0, \lambda_0)$:
\begin{equation}
x_i = N\cos\phi_0\,(\lambda_i-\lambda_0), \qquad y_i = M\,(\phi_i-\phi_0),
\end{equation}
where $M$ and $N$ are the radii of curvature of the Earth at $\phi_0$ in the World Geodetic System 1984. The roof area $A$ and the perimeter $P$ follow from the corners $(x_i, y_i)$:
\begin{equation}
A = \frac12\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i y_{i+1}-x_{i+1} y_i)\Big|, \qquad P=\sum_{i=1}^{n} d_i,
\end{equation}
where $d_i$ is the length of side $i$ and corner $n+1$ is corner 1. For all test buildings, this area is within \ProjErr\% of the exact area on the Earth's surface.

\subsection{Find the Usable Area}
Safety rules ask for a clear strip along the roof edge. We use a strip of width $s=\CfgSetback$~m and shrink the outline inward by $s$ to get the usable area $A_u$. For a simple roof, this gives about
\begin{equation}
A_u \approx A - sP .
\label{eq:pa}
\end{equation}
So a small or narrow building, with a large perimeter for its area, loses more of its roof.

\subsection{Lay Out the Modules}
On a flat roof, the modules stand in tilted rows that face south, as in \cite{gagnon2016}. Each module is 1~m wide and $L=2$~m long, and is tilted by \CfgTilt$^\circ$. The rows need gaps so that one row does not shade the next. We space them by $L/k$, where $k$ is the share of roof covered by modules. With $k=\CfgPack$, the rows are \RowPitch~m apart. At noon on 21 December, the sun is at its lowest of the year (\ShadeElev$^\circ$). The shadow of a row then reaches \ShadeLen~m, less than the \ShadeGap~m gap to the next row (Fig.~\ref{fig:hero}d). On that day, the rows do not shade each other from \ShadeFrom{} to \ShadeTo{}. PVGIS does not model the shading outside these hours. The tool places a module only where its whole footprint fits inside the usable area, and counts the modules $N$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:hero}b). The direct-current (dc) capacity is
\begin{equation}
P_{\mathrm{dc}} = N\,P_{\mathrm{mod}} ,
\end{equation}
where $P_{\mathrm{mod}}=\ModuleW$~W is the rating of one module with an efficiency of \CfgEta\%. A simpler rule, $P_{\mathrm{dc}} = k\,\eta\,A_u \cdot 1~\text{kW/m}^2$, ignores that whole modules do not fill the roof edges. We compare the two in Section~\ref{sec:q2}. An inverter converts the dc power to alternating current (ac); we size it at the dc capacity divided by \CfgDcac.

\subsection{Estimate the Energy}
PVGIS uses satellite data and models the effects of heat and sun angle on the panels \cite{huld2012}. We downloaded its yearly energy per kilowatt of rated module power, $Y$ (kWh per kWp, kilowatt-peak), for all tilts and directions at one site in each emirate. The tool reads $Y$ from these stored tables, so it works offline. The yearly energy is
\begin{equation}
E = P_{\mathrm{dc}}\,Y .
\end{equation}
To check PVGIS, we modeled the same system with pvlib \cite{holmgren2018}, which uses its own weather and panel models. The two tools agreed within \PvlibMax\% for seven tilts and directions.

\subsection{Estimate the Payback}
With an installed cost $c$ per kWp and an electricity price $r$, the simple payback in years is
\begin{equation}
T = \frac{c\,P_{\mathrm{dc}}}{r\,E} = \frac{c}{r\,Y} .
\label{eq:payback}
\end{equation}
The payback does not depend on the roof size. It depends only on cost, price and yield. The tool also reports the net present value (NPV) and the levelized cost of electricity (LCOE) over 25 years. All money values are in UAE dirham (AED).

\subsection{Buildings}
We chose \NBuildings{} flat-roof buildings in \NEmirates{} emirates: \NAcademic{} university buildings, \NCommercial{} shops and malls, and \NIndustrial{} warehouses and workshops (Table~\ref{tab:buildings}). Their OSM areas range from \AreaMin{} to \AreaMax~m$^2$. We saved each OSM outline, so the results do not change when the map is edited.

\subsection{Reference Roof Areas}
No official roof data are public for these buildings, so one author traced every roof by hand on the satellite images in Google Earth. The tracing files showed only a pin at each building, not the OSM outline. Two traces were redrawn after a first check. We then drew every trace and its OSM outline on a satellite image at a known scale (whole Web-Mercator zoom levels only) and checked that the trace followed the roof edge. All \NManual{} traces did. After the comparison, the author looked at the images again and labeled each OSM outline as correct or wrong. A wrong outline got an error type (Table~\ref{tab:failures}). All traces and labels are in the repository.

\subsection{Error Measures}
For each building, the area error is
\begin{equation}
e = \frac{A_{\mathrm{OSM}}-A_{\mathrm{trace}}}{A_{\mathrm{trace}}} .
\end{equation}
We report the mean absolute percentage error (MAPE), the median absolute error and the mean bias error (MBE), which is the mean of $e$. We do not report $R^2$, because the areas range over three orders of magnitude and $R^2$ would be close to 1 for almost any method.

\subsection{Q1: Is the OSM Roof Area Correct?}

Fig.~\ref{fig:failures} shows a correct outline and one example of each error type. Fig.~\ref{fig:validation} shows the error for all buildings, and Table~\ref{tab:buildings} lists the values.

\emph{When the outline is correct.} This was the case for \NOsmOk{} of the \NManual{} buildings. Their MAPE was \OkMape\%, the median error \OkMedape\% and the MBE \OkMbe\%. All errors were between \OkMin\% and \OkMax\%, and \OkWithinFive{} buildings were within 5\%. We found no trend with building size or type.

\emph{When the outline is wrong.} This was the case for \NOsmBad{} of the \NManual{} buildings (\OsmBadPct\%), in four different ways (Table~\ref{tab:failures}, Fig.~\ref{fig:failures}). In \NPartial{} buildings, OSM mapped only one part of the building, so the area was \PartialMin\% to \PartialMax\% too small. In one market, OSM outlined the whole market ground, but only a small shed has a roof. In one office building, the open courtyard was counted as roof. One small building has roofs at several levels, and the hand trace covers only the upper one. Over all \NManual{} buildings, the median error was \ManMedape\%; the mean was \ManMape\% because of the market.

\emph{Second reference.} We also compared the hand traces with building outlines that Microsoft extracted from satellite images by machine learning \cite{msfootprints}. For the \MsmanN{} buildings with a clear match, their median error was \MsmanMedape\%, worse than OSM. Like our traces, they were often a few meters away from the OSM outline (Fig.~\ref{fig:hero}a), which changes the area little.

The answer to Q1 is therefore: when the outline is right, the OSM area is within a few percent, but some outlines are wrong. A user should look at the outline before trusting the result.

\subsection{Q2: What Does a Report Look Like?}
\label{sec:q2}

Fig.~\ref{fig:hero} shows the result for Building W5 at the University of Sharjah. Its OSM outline has \CaseVertices{} corners and an area of \CaseArea~m$^2$, \CaseManErr\% from the hand trace. After the \CfgSetback~m edge strip, \CaseUsable~m$^2$ (\CaseUsablePct\%) is usable. \CaseModules{} modules fit in south-facing rows, which gives \CasePdc~kWp. With \CaseSy~kWh/kWp from PVGIS, the system produces \CaseEnergy~MWh per year. At \CfgCost~AED/kWp and \CfgTariff~AED/kWh, it costs \CaseCapex~AED, saves \CaseSavings~AED per year and pays back in \CasePayback{} years (\CaseDiscPayback{} years when discounted). Over \CfgLife{} years, the NPV is \CaseNpv~AED and the LCOE is \CaseLcoe~AED/kWh. The user runs the same steps in a desktop interface and gets a one-page report (Fig.~\ref{fig:tool}, shown for a warehouse in Dubai).

\emph{Real layout against the simple rule.} The simple rule $k\,A_u$ would allow \CaseModulesRule{} modules on W5, \CaseRuleGap\% more than the layout. The rows run east to west, while the walls of W5 do not, so the triangles at the roof edges stay empty (Fig.~\ref{fig:hero}b). Over all \LcN{} buildings, the layout fits a median of \LcMedian\% of the modules given by the simple rule (range \LcMin--\LcMax\%). The lowest value belongs to the smallest roof. The simple rule therefore overestimates the system size by a few percent on large roofs and by much more on small ones. Such constant factors are common, but they ignore the shape of each roof and are hard to validate \cite{melius2013}.

\subsection{Q3: Which Inputs Change the Answer?}

\emph{Panel direction.} Fig.~\ref{fig:orientation} shows the PVGIS yield for all directions in Sharjah. At \CfgTilt$^\circ$ tilt, south gives \OrSouth\% of the best yield, east \OrEast\%, west \OrWest\% and north \OrNorth\%. Flat panels give \OrFlat\% in every direction. The sun is high in the UAE, so at low tilt the direction matters little. For W5, rows that follow the walls instead of facing south lose only \CaseAlLoss\% of the energy. Across the five emirates, the yield at 15$^\circ$ south ranges from \OrSiteMin{} to \OrSiteMax~kWh/kWp.

\emph{Edge strip.} The usable share of the roof falls with the ratio $P/A$, as \eqref{eq:pa} predicts. It ranges from \SbMin\% for the smallest building to \SbMax\% for the largest. The simple formula \eqref{eq:pa} differs from the exact result by a median of \SbLinMed{} percentage points.

\emph{All inputs.} Fig.~\ref{fig:tornado} changes one input at a time for W5. The energy depends most on the share of roof covered by modules (the row spacing) and the module efficiency. These are design choices, not errors in the data. The payback depends only on cost, price and yield \eqref{eq:payback}, and ranges from \PayMin{} to \PayMax{} years. A 5\% error in roof area changes the energy by about 5\% and does not change the payback.

\section{Discussion}

\emph{Use.} SolarScan suits a first screen of many flat roofs, such as a campus, a group of warehouses or a city's public buildings. It is not a design tool. It estimates the technical potential; what is actually built also depends on owners, rules and the grid \cite{shi2026}. Because the payback does not depend on roof size, an area error changes the size of the opportunity, but rarely the decision to look closer.

\emph{Limitations.} First, OSM gives the outline of the building, not the roof. The satellite image of W5 shows a dome and air-conditioning units on the roof (Fig.~\ref{fig:hero}a). The layout ignores them, as well as skylights, shade from tall neighbors and roof strength, so the real system will be smaller. Second, one person made all traces and labels, after seeing the OSM areas for some buildings. A second, independent tracer would make it stronger. Third, the tool cannot yet detect a wrong outline by itself. Fourth, cost and electricity prices differ between emirates and customers, so users should enter their own values. The default price leaves out the fuel surcharge and value-added tax, so the savings are on the low side. Fifth, PVGIS uses a fixed 14\% loss, and dust in the Gulf can cause larger losses when panels are not cleaned \cite{alali2013}.

\section{Conclusion}

SolarScan gives a free and repeatable first answer to the rooftop solar question for any building in OpenStreetMap. On \NManual{} UAE buildings with hand traces, when the OSM outline was correct (\NOsmOk{} buildings), its area was within \OkMape\% of the trace on average. The other \NOsmBad{} outlines were wrong in different ways. For a typical university building, the tool estimates a \CasePdc~kWp system with a payback of \PayMin{} to \PayMax{} years. Cost and electricity price drive this result, not roof shape or panel direction. Next, we will detect wrong outlines automatically, find roof equipment in open images and test the tool in other Gulf cities.
